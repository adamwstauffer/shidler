## About the course

This course takes you from an idea to a fundable small business in sustainable agriculture, built week by week. By the end of the semester you will have produced four things a lender, grant program, investor or customer would look for:

1. **A Business Plan** for your venture
2. **Basic Financial Statements**: an income statement, cash-flow projection and balance sheet that agree with each other
3. **An Investor Pitch**: a short deck and a recorded presentation
4. **The start of a website**: the pages and the facts a customer, a search engine or an AI assistant would need to find you

You can build your own venture: a farm, a value-added food product, or a service that supports local agriculture. Every student builds their own venture. If you don't have an idea yet, the first two stages help you find one. The course's worked example, a half-acre market garden, shows what each piece looks like when it is done. Each week adds one piece, and the pieces fit together into the final plan.

**Why this course starts with food security.** Hawaiʻi imports most of what it eats, through a supply chain with very little slack. The opening week asks why that makes local, sustainable food production a business opportunity and not only a cause. The rest of the course is about building a business that can seize it.

## What you'll be able to do

- Define a venture's mission, customer and market, and size that market from the ground up
- Design an operation that is sustainable in measurable terms, and claim only what you can back up
- Choose a business model and sales channels by margin, not only by sales volume
- Read and fill in a linked set of financial statements in Excel, and find the cash trough before it finds you
- Identify the legal, tax and risk obligations of a small food or farm business in Hawaiʻi
- Use AI as a reviewer and research assistant while keeping the work your own, and say how you used it
- Pitch the venture, with an ask that comes from your own numbers

## How a week works

1. **Thursday: the lecture is posted.** A recording introduces the week's topic and works an example. Watch it when it suits you. The slides are the reading unless the schedule names another one.
2. **By the Monday before the finished stage is due, 11:59 pm HST: your first pass.** Each stage has a short form on Lamaku with that stage's questions. Answer them in your own words, in rough bullets, **without AI**. This is brainstorming. It does not need to be polished; it needs to be yours and specific.
3. **By Friday, 11:59 pm HST: the finished stage.** Turn your first pass into the plan section, the numbers or the slides the stage brief asks for, and submit it as Lamaku directs.

## AI Use Policy

AI is not required in this course. If you prefer to write all of your work yourself, that is fine and is never penalized. If you do use AI, it is allowed at three stages, in a set order. Any AI tool works, and the free version of any of them is enough.

1. **Your first pass is done without AI.** Every stage starts with your own thinking, in your own words, in that stage's form. You will have the chance to tighten it up with AI later.
2. **AI reviews of your work are allowed at three stages.** Using AI is optional. In Stages 1–3 the finished work is notes (bullets, tables and numbers), with the mission and the values written out in your own sentences. At Stage 4 you may have an AI first join your own notes into paragraphs under a strict prompt, adding nothing, and you check every sentence against your notes; it then reviews that first full draft. At Stage 9 an AI may review the finished plan. At Stage 10 it may help you rehearse and tighten your pitch. Reviewing the whole draft at once is deliberate: an AI is most useful at finding where two sections disagree.
3. **You revise and decide.** You are responsible for every claim and number in your work, and at Stages 4 and 9 you write what you changed and what you rejected.

Between those stages, two small uses are fine once your first pass is in: asking an AI which agency or publisher holds a fact, and asking it to explain a spreadsheet formula.

**Order is what counts.** If an AI session on a stage's work is dated before your first-pass form, the first-pass credit for that stage may be reduced. Nothing is ever taken off on a guess about how you wrote something.

**After the first pass, disclosed AI use is never penalized.** At Stages 4, 9 and 10, say in your stage note which tool you used and what you asked it, and save any chat you relied on as a file, not as a share link. Lamaku explains where to put it.

**AI is a poor source of facts.** Any price, statistic, regulation, grant amount or market figure in your work needs a source you opened yourself, such as an agency page, a published report or a supplier's price list. "An AI said so" is not a source.

## Grading

| Component | What it is | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Discussion posts | Short written posts on Lamaku | 10% |
| Weekly stages | Stages 0–8, in equal shares. Stage 7, the Basic Financial Statements, carries three grades, one for each statement: income statement, cash flow and balance sheet. The first pass counts equally toward each of the three statement grades. | 40% |
| Business Plan | Stage 9, the assembled plan and the "what changed after AI review" memo | 25% |
| Investor Pitch | Stage 10, the deck draft and the recorded pitch | 25% |
| **Total** | | **100%** |

**Each stage is graded in two parts.** The first pass counts for one third of the stage and is credited for being specific and on time, not for polish. A late first pass is accepted with 10% taken off that part. The finished stage counts for the rest and is graded on the rubric in its brief.

Feedback comes in two forms: a full written comment at the major stages (2, 4, 7, 9 and 10), and a score with a short note at the others.

I round your semester grade up to the nearest whole number, so anyone on the cusp gets the higher grade. If you find a scoring error, let me know and I'll correct it.

**Late work** is accepted with 10% taken off. This applies to each part of a stage separately: a late first pass and a late finished stage are each accepted with 10% off that part.

## Featured Videos and Content

**An In-Depth Look at America's Revolutionary Organic Research Trial – Rodale Institute**
Rodale Institute scientists take viewers in the field as they dive-deep into North America's longest running side-by-side comparison of conventional and organic agriculture.

**Regenerative Agriculture on a Small Scale | What it Looks Like – No-Till Growers**
What is regenerative agriculture, what does regenerative agriculture look like, what is small scale regenerative agriculture, using animals on a small scale, and more!

**Ahupuaʻa: Native Hawaiians Taking Back Their Watersheds – Andrew Millison**
Permaculture instructor Andrew Millison journeys to Hawaii to investigate the ecological and cultural restoration work of the Independent Sovereign Nation of Hawaii, an aboriginal organization who are actively rebuilding the traditional Ahupuaʻa watershed-scale agricultural system on the Island of Oahu.

**The Market Gardener: Six Figure Farming – Jean-Martin Fortier**
14 Part Video Series: Innovative organic farmer Jean-Martin Fortier, author of "The Market Gardener" shares his profitable methods for achieving success growing vegetables on a small plot of land.

**High Bionutrient Crop Production – Dan Kittredge**
9 Part Video Series: Dan Kittredge and the Bionutrient Food Association are on a mission to increase quality in the food supply through educating growers, raising awareness among consumers and conducting research on the nature of crop quality and the correlation between soil health, plant health and human health.

**Seed: The Untold Story**
Documentary: In the last century, 94% of our seed varieties have disappeared. As biotech chemical companies control the majority of our seed, farmers and indigenous keepers fight a David and Goliath battle to defend the future of our food.

**Growing Broke: Forever chemicals tainting food supply, destroying US farms – NewsNation**
Two-year investigation into "forever chemicals" contaminating American farmland, impacting our nation's food, and in turn, all of us.

## Instructor Background

I am a Faculty Lecturer in finance and economics at the Shidler College of Business, University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa, where I teach undergraduate, MBA and executive MBA courses in Honolulu, Vietnam and online. I also teach sustainable agriculture entrepreneurship at Windward Community College.

I teach from practice. I was a trader and market-maker at Lehman Brothers and then Barclays Capital in New York, on the desk responsible for all U.S.-listed Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs). There I priced and traded international and commodity ETFs, managed real-time risk on more than $1 billion in inventory, and built pricing and carry-arbitrage algorithms. I later served as founder and Chief Investment Officer of Springline Capital, a boutique Registered Investment Advisor in the British Virgin Islands, and as Director and Portfolio Manager at OIA Ltd. Before my MBA, I was a Marketing Manager at Advent Software, where I launched its global solution for hedge funds and offshore fund administrators.

Every course project I teach is built around one problem: AI now drafts the first pass that junior analysts used to build, and that first pass was where they learned judgment. My students frame the question, specify what a correct model must do, build it with AI, audit the result and defend the decision, all in the open on GitHub.

I hold an MBA in Finance from The Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania and a BA in Biology from Trinity College. After surviving Hurricane Irma in the British Virgin Islands, I founded Greenshoot.org, a disaster-recovery registry that connected survivors with donors. I am also establishing a regenerative organic food forest on former pastureland.

Source: [Adam W. Stauffer, full bio](https://adamwstauffer.github.io/bio.html), fetched November 18, 2026.
